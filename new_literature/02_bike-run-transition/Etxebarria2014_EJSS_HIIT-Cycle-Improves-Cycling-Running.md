# High-intensity cycle interval training improves cycling and running performance in triathletes

NAROA ETXEBARRIA<sup>1,2</sup>, JUDITH M. ANSON<sup>2</sup>, DAVID B. PYNE<sup>2,3</sup>, & RICHARD A. FERGUSON<sup>1</sup> 

<sup>1</sup>School of Sport, Exercise and Health Sciences, Loughborough University, Loughborough, UK, <sup>2</sup>National Institute of Sports Studies (NISS), Faculty of Health, University of Canberra, Canberra, Australia, <sup>3</sup>Department of Physiology, Australian Institute of Sport, Canberra, Australia 

## Abstract

Effective cycle training for triathlon is a challenge for coaches We compared the effects of two variants of cycle high: intensity interval training (HIT) on triathlon-specific cycling and running. Fourteen moderately-trained male triathletes $( \dot { V } \mathbf { O } _ { 2 \mathrm { p e a k } } 5 8 . 7 \pm 8 . 1 $ mL kg<sup>−1</sup> min<sup>−1</sup>; mean ± SD) completed on separate occasions a maximal incremental test $( \dot { V } \mathbf { O } _ { 2 \mathrm { p e a k } }$ and maximal aerobic power). 16 × 20 s cycle sprints and a 1-h triathlon-specific cycle followed immediately by a 5 km run time trial. Participants were then pair-matched and assigned randomly to either a long high-intensity interval training (LONG) (6–8 × 5 min efforts) or short high-intensity interval training (SHORT) (9–11 × 10, 20 and 40 s efforts) HIT cycle training intervention. Six training sessions were completed over 3 weeks before participants repeated the baseline testing. Both groups had an ∼7% increase in $\dot { V } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 \mathrm { p e a k } }$ (SHORT 7.3%, ±4.6%; mean, ±90% confidence limits; LONG 7.5%, ±1.7%). There was a moderate improvement in mean power for both the SHORT (10.3%, ±4.4%) and LONG (10.7%, ±6.8%) groups during the last eight 20-s sprints. There was a small to moderate decrease in heart rate, blood lactate and perceived exertion in both groups during the 1-h triathlon-specific cycling but only the LONG group had a substantial decrease in the subsequent 5-km run time (64, ±59 s). Moderately-trained triathletes should use both short and long high-intensity intervals to improve cycling physiology and performance. Longer 5-min intervals on the bike are more likely to benefit 5 km running performance. 

Keywords: Variable power, Olympic distance triathlon, endurance, cycling 

## Introduction

Olympic distance triathlon is an ∼2-h endurance sport and all three sections (swim, cycle, run) require highly developed aerobic fitness. The large number of near-maximal sprints during the ∼1-h triathlon cycling section have become more prominent in recent years due to drafting, individual and team tactics employed, varying terrain and criterium-style cycle courses with tight technical corners. These characteristics make for a highly variable power profile including supramaximal, above maximal aerobic power (MAP), and other high-intensity efforts in races (Bernard et al., 2009), differentiating it from other events such as time trialling or road cycling. Power variations are often abrupt due to continuous accelerations and decelerations, especially in a technical course with dynamic race tactics. 

Cycling $\dot { V } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 \mathrm { p e a k } }$ is one of the physiological variable that best correlates $( r = 0 . 8 6 )$ with Olympic distance triathlon performance (Bailey, Pearce, Etxebarria, & Ingham, 2007). Therefore, increasing ${ \dot { V } } \mathbf { O } _ { 2 \mathrm { p e a k } }$ and subsequently maximal power output is an important goal for triathletes. Increasing an athlete’s maximal capacity could translate to lowering the relative intensity of the cycle section, allow a higher power output during cycling and/or benefit the subsequent running section in Olympic distance triathlon. Consequently, given that the start of a run after triathlon-specific cycling is negatively affected by variations in power of preceding cycling (Etxebarria, Anson, Pyne, & Ferguson, 2013), lower relative cycling intensities (at the same absolute workload) would give triathletes an advantage of entering the subsequent run with less relative fatigue. High power output efforts often deteriorate during the latter stages of triathlon cycling (Bernard et al., 2009), for which improving repeat sprint ability would be favourable. Improving maximal aerobic capacity could also aid the recovery rate between high-intensity efforts (Tomlin & Wenger, 2001) and/ or minimise decay in maximal power during repeated sprints over time. 

Due to the recently described triathlon-specific physiological responses and physical demands (Etxebarria, Anson, et al., 2013; Etxebarria, Hunt, Ingham, & Ferguson, 2013), studying training interventions to improve performance is becoming increasingly important. Aerobic capacity as well as cycling repeat sprint ability as a consequence of closed-circuit style cycling (Ebert, Martin, Stephens, & Withers, 2006) are important aspects of triathlon racing. However, training for a multidisciplinary sport such as triathlon is time demanding and a challenge. High-intensity interval training (HIT) can be used to improve physical fitness in less time, and with reduced training volume, than traditional endurance training (Hawley, Myburgh, Noakes, & Dennis, 1997). This training method is commonly employed in individual sports like running and cycling, but little is known about the best HIT program to improve performance in multidisciplinary events such as triathlon. 

The HIT approach to endurance training stratifies training from very easy to very hard intensities as a key element of an optimal training strategy for already trained athletes competing in endurance events (Laursen, 2010). HIT also allows athletes to accumulate time at higher training intensities, which is difficult to achieve when training in a continuous manner (Billat et al., 2000). In addition, the time efficiency of HIT suits a multidisciplinary sport such as triathlon. The specificity and degree of adaptation to HIT depends, however, on the number of repetitions and sessions and duration and intensity of the efforts (Gibala, Little, Macdonald, & Hawley, 2012). It is unclear what form of HIT would best produce triathlon-specific adaptations and to our knowledge, no previous study has directly examined the effect of HIT on both cycling and running performance in triathletes. 

HIT training can take the form of either short (∼30 s) or longer (5 min) intervals. Some of the early work done in well-trained cyclists using 5-min interval training reported an ∼5% increase in $\dot { V } \mathbf { O } _ { 2 \mathrm { p e a k } }$ by Hawley et al. (1997), and ∼4% in maximal power output (Lindsay et al., 1996). In addition, both short (Stepto, Hawley, Dennis, & Hopkins, 1999) and longer (Lindsay et al., 1996; Stepto et al., 1999) intervals improved ∼1 h cycling time trial performance by up to 8% in well-trained athletes. However, the physiological demands of a self-paced cycle are markedly different to the variable power cycle section of a triathlon race (Bentley, Millet, Vleck, & McNaughton, 2002; Bernard et al., 2009). 

Given that triathletes undertake multiple shortduration and high-intensity efforts in races, we developed a training program to target triathlonspecific efforts. We sought to determine whether repeat short (10, 20 and 40 s) interval efforts would yield similar improvements for triathlon compared with the established long 5-min interval format employed in cycling. Consequently, we investigated the effects of six sessions of short and long HIT strategies on both physiological and performance outcomes in cycling and subsequent 5 km running. 

## Methods

## Participants

A total of 14 moderately-trained male triathletes participated in this controlled laboratory-based investigation. The subjects’ physical characteristics were: age 33 ± 8 years, height $1 . 8 2 \pm 0 . 0 8 ~ \mathrm { m } ,$ body mass $7 7 . 6 \pm 9 . 7 \mathrm { k g } , \dot { V } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 \mathrm { p e a k } } 4 . 5 0 \pm 0 . 4 6 \mathrm { L } \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }$ or $5 8 . 7 \ \pm \ 8 . 1$ mL kg<sup>−1</sup> min<sup>−1</sup> (mean ± SD). Participants had 2–3 years of experience in cycle and run training, were mostly competing in non-drafting races and provided a diary of their training activities outside of the experimental training intervention. Participants were instructed to abstain from any physical exercise, caffeine or alcohol and replicate the same dietary practice in the 24 h prior to each experimental trial. The study was approved by the Loughborough University Ethics Advisory Committee and the Committee for Ethics in Human Research at the University of Canberra. All participants signed written informed consent to participate in the study. 

## Baseline and post-training testing protocols

All experimental data collection was carried out in spring after winter training. Participants performed three different experimental trials on separate occasions, each separated by at least 48 h. First, participants performed a maximal incremental test to determine MAP and $\dot { V } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 \mathrm { p e a k } }$ on an electromagnetically braked cycle ergometer (Excalibur Sport, Lode, Netherlands) adjusted to their usual bike position. After a 10-min warm-up at 125 watts (W) the starting power for the test was 150 W with increments of 5 W every 15 s until volitional exhaustion. 

On a separate day repeat sprint ability was assessed on a custom-built wind-braked cycle ergometer (Australian Institute of Sport (AIS), Canberra, Australia) and fitted with the triathletes’ own pedal system and adjusted to their usual bike position. Following a 10-min warm-up at 125 W participants performed 4 sets of 4 × 20 s maximal sprints (16 in total) with 40 s active recovery (pedalling at 50–75 W) between efforts. There was an 8 min rest after the first 8 sprints, and 3 min between the 1st–2nd and 3rd–4th sets. Participants were required to undertake the first 8 sprints at a workload equivalent to 150% MAP to ensure that they were able to successfully complete the last 8 sprints. 

Finally, participants performed a 1-h variable power cycle (Figure 1) and a subsequent 5 km running time trial on a 2 lap outdoors road course (2500 m each lap). The 1-h cycle trial was performed on the same electromagnetically braked cycle ergometer used for the MAP test. The 1-h trial was performed at a mean power output of 65% MAP, simulating the relative intensity observed during Olympic distance triathlon racing (Le Meur et al., 2009), and involved a total of 30 high-intensity (85– 140% MAP) efforts of different intensity and duration: 10 s at 140% MAP, 40 s at 110% MAP, 90 s at 85% MAP, 20 s at 120% MAP and 30 s at 130% MAP. These efforts were alternated with 60 or 70 s bouts of lower-intensity periods (40–60% MAP). All three tests, the MAP, repeat sprints, and 1-h cycle followed by a 5 km run, were repeated in the same order 5–6 days after the training intervention concluded. The variable power protocol was performed at the same absolute power output as at baseline. All the performance outcomes are similar to workouts the participants routinely undertake during training, and therefore no familiarisation trials were deemed necessary.

## Training interventions

Once the baseline testing was performed, participants were pair matched for MAP and body mass, and allocated randomly to either the short highintensity interval training (SHORT) experimental group $( n ~ = ~ 7 )$ or the long high-intensity interval training (LONG) active control group $( n = 7 )$ . Each group completed 6 sessions over 3 weeks (2 sessions a week) of either SHORT or LONG. We evaluated the effectiveness of the SHORT interval training intervention using the LONG interval training as an active control treatment. Both training programmes were delivered in a progressive manner whereby the load increased every week. The participants were asked to abstain from any other high-intensity work during their training program and instructed to continue with their usual training. The supervised training sessions were undertaken on a stationary cycle ergometer (Wattbike, Nottingham, UK – Version 2.50.49, 1–5% typical error in power output). Each subject used the same ergometer for the six sessions, only for training purposes. 

All participants performed a 10-min warm-up at ∼125 W including 3 × 10 s near maximal efforts (SHORT group) and a 45 s sustained effort (LONG group). The SHORT group then performed a set of 3 repetitions of maximal efforts (10, 20 and 40 s) with a 1:3 work/recovery ratio, a set of 3 repetitions with a 1:2 work/recovery ratio, and a third set of 3 repetitions with a 1:2 work/recovery ratio in the first week. In the second week an extra repetition of the 1:3 set was added and in week 3 an extra repetition of the 1:2 set. The LONG group completed $6 \times 5$ min efforts at 80% $\dot { V } \mathbf { O } _ { 2 \mathrm { p e a k } }$ power with 1 min recovery between efforts in week 1, $7 \times 5$ min in week 2 and 8 × 5 min in week 3. 

## Anthropometric and physiological measures

Height and body mass were recorded on the first visit to the laboratory. Expired air was collected during the maximal progressive cycling test by a custom-built open-circuit indirect calorimetry system described previously (Saunders, Pyne, Telford, & Hawley, 2004). The sampling rate was set to 30 s and the mean of the two highest consecutive readings used to determine an individual’s $\dot { V } \mathbf { O } _ { 2 \mathrm { p e a k } }$ during the maximal progressive test. 

Heart rate (HR) and rating of perceived exertion (RPE) were recorded along with a 5-µL capillary blood sample for lactate analysis withdrawn from a fingertip at 20, 40 and 60 min of the 1-h cycle test, and at the end of the maximal progressive cycle test except for RPE. A blood sample was also taken immediately after the 5 km run-time trial. HR was recorded using a Polar HR monitoring system (Polar Heart Rate Monitor, Kempele, Finland) and blood lactate concentration was analysed with a Lactate Pro analyser (Arkray, Kyoto, Japan). RPE was recorded using a scale of 0 (no exertion) to 10 (maximal exertion). At the end of the 5 km run, participants were asked to rate their effort on a scale between 0 and 100%, with 0% representing ‘no effort at all’, and 100% representing ‘giving absolutely everything’. Participants also used a visual analogue scale of 1–5 to indicate how they felt physically with 1 representing ‘terrible’ and 5 ‘fantastic’ (AIS, Effort-Sensation Scale, Version 2.10, January 2010). 

## Statistical analysis

Descriptive data are shown as mean ± SD. An analytical approach determining practical/clinical significance of effects using magnitude-based inferences and precision of estimation was employed (Hopkins, Marshall, Batterham, & Hanin, 2009). Precision of estimation was indicated with 90% confidence limits (CL). The difference between the two groups at any given point in time was expressed as a percentage of baseline score via analysis of logtransformed values in order to reduce bias arising from non-uniformity of error. Standardised difference scores or the effect size between the groups were interpreted according to the following criteria: <0.2 trivial, 0.2–0.6 small, 0.6–1.2 moderate, 1.2–2.0 large and >2.0 very large (Hopkins, 2000). An effect was inferred to be unclear when its confidence interval spanned both substantially positive (+0.2) and substantially negative (−0.2) values. An effect was inferred to be substantial whenever a difference was meaningful and not trivial. Pearson’s correlation analysis was used to measure the degree ofassociation between different physiological and performance markers using a scale of magnitudes (Cohen, 1988): <0.1 trivial, 0.1–0.3 small, 0.3–0.5 moderate, 0.5–0.7 large. A correlation was deemed unclear if the confidence interval spanned both −0.1 and +0.1 values. 

## Results

## SHORT and LONG training interventions

Participants avoided high-intensity exercise outside of the training interventions as indicated by their training diaries and each followed their typical (individual) routine of running (2–5 h $\mathrm { w e e k } ^ { - 1 } )$ During the training period participants in the LONG group completed their 5-min efforts at a mean power output of 284 ± 38 W $( \mathrm { m e a n } \pm \mathrm { s D } )$ Although participants performed one extra 5 min effort every 2 sessions, they maintained a similar mean power output for the 3 weeks (1st week; 283 ± 32 W, 2nd week; 283 ± 40 W and 3rd week; 286 ± 44 W, equivalent to ∼80% $\dot { V } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 \mathrm { p e a k } } )$ . The SHORT group, despite the progressive increase in the number of sprints over the 6 sessions, had $1 \sim 2 1 \% , \pm 1 1 \%$ (mean, ±90% confident limits) increase in 10 s maximal mean power output from week 1 to week 3 (621 ± 46 to 757 ± 101 W), while mean power for the 20 (485 ± 50 W, mean ± SD) and $4 0 \mathrm { ~ s ~ } ( 4 3 5 \pm 3 4$ W) maximal power efforts remained relatively stable over the 6 HIT sessions. There were no training aberrations in their diaries that would explain the differences between the two groups during their post-training testing

The mean power output during the 1-h variable power cycle prior to the 5-km run at baseline and after training was 256 ± 23 W (mean ± SD) for the SHORT group and 255 ± 33 W for the LONG group. The $\mathtt { B _ { L a } }$ and HR levels during the 1-h cycle were moderately lower at 40 and 60 min after the short interval training intervention, and at 20, 40 and 60 min after the long interval intervention (Figure 3). Similarly, the RPE was moderately lower at 20, 40 and 60 min after LONG, but only moderately lower at 60 min for the SHORT group after the training intervention. The LONG group showed a substantial (42, ±33 s, mean, ±90% CL) improvement at the 2.5 km mark and a $6 4 ,$ ±59 s faster overall run time over 5 km after the training intervention. The improvement in run time for the SHORT group was trivial (Table I) with an unclear difference between the groups in 5 km run time. The SHORT group gave 94 (pre) and 97 (post) and the LONG group gave 94 (pre) and 94 (post) out of 100% on the run, and both groups reported feeling OK (3/5) on the run both before and after the training interventions. The baseline 5 km run time (combined data for short and long groups) was negatively correlated with the improvement in 5 km run time after the training interventions (r = −0.42, −0.74 to 0.05), suggesting that the less-fit runners recorded larger improvements. 

## Discussion

This study demonstrated that 3 weeks of HIT involving either short or long cycle intervals (active control) induced small to moderate improvements in $\dot { V } \mathbf { O } _ { 2 \mathrm { p e a k } } ,$ cycling MAP and maximal repeat sprint ability in moderately-trained triathletes. Longer interval training (5 min efforts) improved 5 km run time after a 1-h triathlon-specific variable power cycle by 64 s. The primary performance difference between the short and long training interventions was that only the LONG group substantially improved 5 km run time after 1-h variable cycling. We observed a small improvement in 5 km running performance in the LONG group despite the low intensity and volume of running completed during the 3 weeks of cycle interval training. Notably the improvement in running ability was greater at the 2.5 km time point than at 5 km. Recent research shows that most of the time loss during running after cycling at triathlon-specific variable power occurs in the first half of a 10-km run (Etxebarria, Anson, et al., 2013). Consequently, undertaking long interval training appears to attenuate fatigue during cycling and minimise performance decrements during the early stages of the subsequent run.

Improvements in maximal aerobic capacity after both cycling HIT (Hawley & Noakes, 1992) and running HIT (Billat et al., 2000) could relate to an athlete accumulating long periods of time at or near ${ \dot { V } } \mathbf { O } _ { 2 \mathrm { p e a k } }$ intensity compared with continuous exercise training. Both HIT training interventions increased $\dot { V } \mathbf { O } _ { 2 \mathrm { p e a k } } ,$ which was accompanied by a similar increase in maximal power output in our triathletes. The ∼7% improvement in $\dot { V } \mathbf { O } _ { 2 \mathrm { p e a k } }$ with SHORT interval training confirms the results of an earlier study in untrained participants $( \dot { V } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 \mathrm { p e a k } } 2 . 8 \pm $ 0.2 L min<sup>−1</sup>) using 30-s high-intensity intervals (Burgomaster et al., 2008). Our data suggest that short-interval high-intensity training is still a potent stimulus for adaptation, even in moderately-trained triathletes. 

The ∼11-W increase in MAP in the LONG group is consistent with other studies using 5-min work bouts (Westgarth-Taylor et al., 1997). The 6% increase in MAP by the SHORT group supports the ∼3% improvement in peak power output previously observed in well-trained athletes following a similar short-interval intervention (Laursen, Shing, Peake, Coombes, & Jenkins, 2005). In contrast, improved time trial performance in the absence of an improved MAP (Stepto et al., 1999) has been reported. Variable findings on MAP between studies likely reflect methodological differences in length (10, 20 and 40 s vs. 30 s) and recovery period between efforts as HIT adaptations are durationspecific (Gibala et al., 2012). The larger increase in MAP observed in our study may relate to the lower physiological profile, and potentially greater room for improvement of our participants in this study $( \dot { V } \mathbf { O } _ { 2 \mathrm { p e a k } } 5 8 . 7 \pm 8 . 1 $ mL $\bar { \mathbf { k } } \mathbf { g } ^ { - 1 } \mathbf { \epsilon } \mathbf { m i n } ^ { - 1 } )$ , compared to the well-trained athletes $( \dot { V } \mathbf { O } _ { 2 \mathrm { p e a k } } ~ 6 4 . 5 ~ \pm ~ 5 . 2 $ mL $\mathbf { k g } ^ { - 1 }$ min<sup>−1</sup>) in the study by Laursen and colleagues (2005). 

Both HIT strategies had a positive influence on running ability but only in the LONG group was this difference substantial. There was a positive association based on all 14 participants between baseline slower run times and larger improvements after the intervention. However, given the similar pre-training run times and SD for both groups, the substantial improvement of only the LONG group can be attributed to the training intervention. A crosstraining effect between cycling and running allows triathletes to obtain physiological capacities (Hue, Le Gallais, Chollet, & Prefaut, 2000) often comparable to those achieved in individual sports (Billat, Demarle, Slawinski, Paiva, & Koralsztein, 2001; Lucia, Joyos, & Chicharro, 2000) without concomitant doubling of training load. At the same HR, cardiac output is lower in cycling compared to running due to a lower stroke volume (Faulkner, Roberts, Elk, & Conway, 1971). Therefore, for central adaptations to transfer to running in response to cycle training, the cycling intensity needs to be near maximal as was the case in our study. Central adaptations such as higher ${ \dot { V } } \mathbf { O } _ { 2 \mathrm { p e a k } }$ and a metabolic shift from carbohydrate to fat oxidation (Westgarth-Taylor et al., 1997) could explain the transfer of improvements in cycling induced by our longer HIT sessions to improved running ability. The residual effects of prior cycling are highest during the first lap (2.5 km) of the run (Heiden & Burnett, 2003). The majority (67%) of the improvement after the LONG intervention occurred during the first 2.5 km. 

The small to moderately lower perceptual and physiological responses during the 1-h variable power cycle protocol is presumably related to an increased maximal aerobic capacity, and consequently lower relative intensity. The 1-h cycle intensity used in our study is similar to previous literature reported (63% MAP) during an international triathlon race (Bernard et al., 2009; Le Meur et al., 2009). The sport-specific cycle protocol used in our study mirrors the demands endured in field triathlon racing with its frequent and intermittent supramaximal (>MAP) efforts and considerable (15%) time spent above MAP intensity (Bernard et al., 2009). By keeping the absolute power output the same after the training intervention we were able to demonstrate that improvements in maximal capacity and sprint ability can reduce the physiological cost of cycling in triathlon racing. The 1-h variable power cycle replicating the race demands in triathlon makes our results applicable to the sport.

The substantially higher power output after the HIT sessions should give a triathlete an advantage over hilly or technical sections demanding sudden power surges. Short-duration sprints are highly dependent on the anaerobic (phosphocreatine and glycolytic) pathways to generate energy. However, the contribution of aerobic energy increases greatly as the number of sprints increases (Gaitanos, Williams, Boobis, & Brooks, 1993). The increase in maximal aerobic capacity presumably influenced the improvement in repeat sprint ability by aiding phosphocreatine resynthesis between repeat sprints (Harris et al., 1976). Higher aerobic capacity also contributes to energy production in the later maximal efforts (Bogdanis, Nevill, Boobis, & Lakomy, 1996). Moreover, longer high-intensity intervals increase intracellular buffering capacity in skeletal muscle (Weston et al., 1997) and lactate and $\mathrm { H } ^ { + }$ removal (Juel et al., 2004), which could explain improved repeat sprint ability (Mohr et al., 2007). Both short and long HIT sessions improved repeat sprint ability, although different physiological adaptations might underpin the observed improvements after both training interventions. Future studies should investigate physiological mechanisms explaining the transfer of improvements from cycling to running and extend the work reported here to a complete swim-cycle-run. 

## Practical implications

Different HIT interventions are successful in inducing desirable training adaptations due to the combination of high- and low-intensity work. Coaches, sports scientists and moderately-trained triathletes should consider the benefits of a brief program of cycling HIT in the lead-up to competition to improve performance with a minimal volume of training while maintaining intensity, an ideal scenario for pre-competition or taper phases. Triathletes seeking improvements in both cycling and running should consider longer high-intensity intervals to maximise training adaptation (with shorter interval training best for athletes seeking improvements in only cycling adaptations). Triathletes coming off a well-designed cycling and running training program could benefit from a faster start to the subsequent run section due to a less fatigue accumulated during cycling. 

## Conclusions

Implementation of HIT offers specific performance improvements for cycling and running. In a race situation, improvements in repeat sprint ability should translate to an enhanced ability to gain or maintain positions during surges, uphill or technical sections in the latter stages of the cycle section when other competitors might be fatiguing and dropping back in the field. As few as six sessions of short and long HIT efforts substantially enhance selected physiological and performance measures of triathlon performance such as repeat sprint ability, and lower physiological disturbances and perceived exertion during the 1-h triathlon-specific cycle section in moderately-trained triathletes. Long interval training also improves 5 km running ability.
