# A Paradigm Shift in Understanding Overuse Running-Related Injuries: Findings From the Garmin-RUNSAFE Study Point to a Sudden Not Gradual Onset

i OBJECTIVES: To investigate the proportion of running-related injuries that can be classified as (1) being based on overload with an acute mechanism with a sudden onset, (2) being overuse in the sense they are based on a repetitive mechanism with a sudden onset; and (3) being overuse in the sense that runners gradually develop problems that lead to an injury. 

i DESIGN: Cohort study with an 18-month follow-up. 

i METHODS: English-speaking runners aged at least 18 years who were tracking running activities were included. Running-related injury during follow-up categorized into 3 groups: (1) those with overload injury, (2) those with a running-related problem 7 to 28 days prior to reporting a runningrelated injury, and (3) those sustaining a runningrelated injury without any preceding problems. 

i RESULTS: Overload injury accounted for 467 of 1666 injuries (28%), while 1199 injuries were overuse (72%). Among overuse injuries, the proportion of runners having a problem prior to injury occurrence was 6.9% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 5.5%, 8.4%) 7 days prior to injury and 11.1% (95% CI: 9.4%, 13.0%) 28 days prior to injury.

Although debatable,5,79,21 sports injuries may be divided into 2 types, traditionally distinguished by their temporal origin: overload and overuse. Overload injuries—referred to as sports injuries caused by a single, identifiable traumatic event—commonly occur suddenly during a training session or competition.<sup>1</sup> In 

contrast, overuse sports injuries are caused by a process of gradual onset characterized by an underlying pathogenesis of 

repetitive microtrauma.<sup>13</sup> While it is accepted that overload injuries occur suddenly, with the athlete being able to recall a specific moment in time in which the injury occurred, little to no research has explored the gradual onset hypothesis, including what constitutes “gradual” in terms of time or exposure. 

In certain contexts, overuse injuries may manifest initially as self-reported problems or “impairments” but are not necessarily nor definitively classified as injuries.<sup>21</sup> Over time, such problems may gradually develop into an injury, defined either subjectively through athlete self-reporting and/or objectively by, eg, diagnostic imaging and/or biomarkers. While the suggestion is plausible and echoes the experiences of some runners, overuse injuries do not always follow a pattern of preceding problems, whether subjectively or objectively reported. For instance, overuse injuries could occur suddenly during a single session with an unusually high intensity or training volume. Consider an athlete who is used to running 2 to 3 miles per session. If this runner participates in a marathon and sustains an injury during the event, the body undergoes an excessive load in 1 session rather than gradually over multiple sessions. This challenges the suggested theory in research and clinical contexts that overuse injuries develop gradually over time. 

It is plausible that the “too much” period of development of overuse injuries is not strictly confined to a gradual onset; some may develop suddenly from a single training session that radically exceeds the norm, while others do develop gradually over 1 or more sessions of participation due to consistently excessive training loads. This idea aligns with the consensus statement by Bahr et al<sup>2</sup> that suggests 3 diferent onsets underpinning sports injury development: (1) overload injury with acute mechanism with a sudden onset, (2) overuse injury with repetitive mechanism with a sudden onset, and (3) overuse injury repetitive mechanism with a gradual onset. Yet, the evidence that supports the idea that some overuse injuries occur suddenly without prior problems (repetitive mechanism with a sudden onset) while others develop gradually with preceding problems (repetitive mechanism with a gradual onset) is in its infancy within a running injury context. 

The main purpose of our exploratory study was to use data from a running injury context to investigate the proportion of running-related injuries that can be classified as (1) being based on overload with an acute mechanism with a sudden onset, (2) being overuse in the sense they are based on a repetitive mechanism with a sudden onset and (3) being overuse in the sense that runners gradually develop problems that over time becomes injurious. 

## METHODS

TCommittee Central Denmark Re- gions (request number: 227/2016; record number: 1-10-72-189-16). We used data from the Garmin-RUNSAFE Running Health Study. The Garmin-RUNSAFE Running Health Study aimed to explore the “too much, too soon” theory in relation to RRI occurrence by conducting an 18-month observational cohort study.<sup>17,18</sup> The inclusion criteria involved runners who consented to upload data from their running sessions to Garmin Connect, a global web-based training diary (https://connect.garmin.com).<sup>17</sup> According to the power calculation described in the Garmin-RUNSAFE Running Health Study protocol, 9571 runners were required to constitute the cohort.<sup>17</sup> With reference to the Danish Act on Research of Health Projects, Section 14, no. 2, permission from the research ethical committees was not required. Data collection and data storage procedures were approved by The Danish Data Protection Agency.<sup>17</sup> Before inclusion, all participants signed an online informed consent form. Purpose, recruitment strategy and inclusion, and exclusion criteria of the original study have been described elsewhere.<sup>17</sup> 

## Study Population

Included runners were owners of a Garmin watch with running activity tracking enabled and held a Garmin Connect account, to which they were required to upload their data from running sessions during follow-up. Runners were recruited between July 2019 and November 2019, inclusive. The follow-up period ended in December 2020. Thus, the individual follow-up period varied between the participants. All runners were ≥18 years of age and possessed adequate English language comprehension to understand the recruitment material, consent form, and questionnaires presented in English. All runners at diferent ability levels were eligible for inclusion, from novice and recreational runners to elite-level runners. 

## Variables

The outcome was defined as a runningrelated injury sustained during followup and was categorized into one of 3 groups: (1) those with an overload injury (traumatic) based on an acute mechanism with a sudden onset; (2) those with repetitive overuse injury with a sudden onset, which in the present article were defined as those with a runningrelated problem  7 to 28 days prior to reporting a running-related overuse injury; and (3) those with repetitive gradual onset overuse injury defined as those sustaining a running-related overuse injury without any preceding problems. 

Data regarding the runners’ injury status was collected through weekly questionnaires automatically sent via email on Sundays. From the question, “In the past week, have you had a musculoskeletal injury or have you experienced a problem to muscles, tendons or bones that is fully or partly caused by running?”, runners were asked to classify themselves into one of 3 categories: (1) injury-free, (2) uninjured but with problems, or (3) injured. A problem was defined as painful and irritating, but less severe than an injury and runners should be able to continue running activities in full. Conversely, an injury was defined as more severe than a problem and something painful and irritating, leading to a reduction in running activity (eg, volume, intensity, frequency). These definitions were introduced based on work by Yamato et al23 regarding the consensus definition of time loss–based running injuries and the reduced-capacity injury definition presented in the Oslo Trauma Research Center questionnaire.<sup>3</sup> Those reporting an injury were subsequently asked whether their injury was traumatic or nontraumatic. Examples for option “traumatic” stated in parentheses were “torsion, fall, blow” while examples for option “nontraumatic” were “overuse, gradual onset.” 

Participating runners could report multiple injuries during follow-up. For our study, the analysis was restricted to first-time injuries. Problems prior to injury were calculated through 2 distinct approaches: (1) any problem in the lower extremity or back regardless of the anatomical location of the injury and (2) problems reported within the same anatomical location where the athlete experienced the reported injury.

## Statistical Methods

The estimated proportion, including 95% confidence interval (CI), reporting a problem prior to sustaining an injury was calculated by dividing the number of injured runners who reported a problem within 4 weeks, equivalent to 28 days (1-28 days), prior to the injury by the total number of injured runners (ie, those experiencing first-time injuries). The proportion reporting a problem 3 weeks (1-21 days), 2 weeks (1-14 days), or 1 week (1-7 days) prior to injury was calculated using the same approach. The distribution of injuries was calculated as the number of injuries within each anatomical location divided by the total number of injuries within a specified anatomical region. All analyses were performed using STATA-MP 17.0.

## DISCUSSION

Wportion of running-related in- portion of running-related injuries that could be classified as overload injuries, overuse injuries that develop gradually (based on a repetitive mechanism with a gradual onset), defined as pre-existing problems in the same anatomical location up to 28 days prior to injury occurrence, and those that occur suddenly without any preceding problems (repetitive mechanism with a sudden onset). Among the overuse inju-

ries, our findings indicate that only up to 12% of the participating runners reported pre-existing problems in the same anatomical location before experiencing a running-related overuse injury. This suggests that most overuse injuries appear to occur without warning. This result further highlights the complexities underpinning injury development within the running context as dichotomizing injury onset onto 2 groups acute and overuse injury may be too simple. Instead, runners, coaches, clinicians, and researchers need to consider 3 diferent modes of onset of running injury development as suggested by Bahr et al<sup>2</sup> (1) overload injury based on an acute mechanism with a sudden onset accounting for 467 of 1666 injuries (28%) in the present dataset, (2) overuse injury with repetitive mechanism with a sudden onset accounting for 1065 of 1666 injuries (64%) in the present dataset, and (3) overuse injury repetitive mechanism with a gradual onset accounting for 134 of 1666 injuries (8%) in the present dataset. 

With these new insights, the monitoring, evaluation, and estimation of injury incidence within a specified running population is a challenging and highly involved process.<sup>11</sup> Our findings challenge sports injury causal theories that suggest overuse injuries predominantly develop gradually over time. It may therefore be necessary to reconsider how best to calculate changes in running training loads given that a majority of injuries in the present study occurred suddenly, within a single running session.<sup>10,12,16,22</sup> 

In a research context, week-to-week ratio<sup>4,8,14</sup> or the acute:chronic workload ratio<sup>6</sup> is used as calculation approaches in the attempt to identify sudden spikes in training load prior to injury occurrence. Based on our findings, most overuse injuries are likely to occur based on a sudden repetitive onset (eg, in 1 running session), complementary calculations may be needed to investigate if single-session spikes in training load increases the risk of sustaining an overuse-related injury compared with a period preceding this training session. Future studies should investigate whether the running distance (or other measures of training load)<sup>19</sup> in the session prior to injury occurrence is vastly greater than the distance in the session in the period 1 to 3 weeks prior to injury occurrence as done in the week-toweek ratio and in the acute:chronic workload ratio. 

The primary strengths of the present study include the cohort study design that allowed for weekly reporting of musculoskeletal problems and injury among a considerably large sample of runners from 87 countries around the world.<sup>15</sup> Another strength is the finding that 467 runners sustained injuries based on an acute mechanism with a sudden onset. These revealed a minority, ranging from 1% to 4%, to report problems in the same anatomical location prior to injury occurrence (APPENDIX B). These proportions are somehow expected as overload injuries are described as occurring suddenly during a training session or competition. Accordingly, runners can correctly classify the problem type. 

## Limitations

First, the methodology used to categorize the gradual development of overuserelated injuries, particularly those identified prior to actual injury occurrence, requires validation. Any conclusions drawn in this study are a product of runners’ subjective accounts of problems and injuries, rather than from objective measurements of injury indicators and other biological markers of pathophysiological change or trauma at the subclinical level. Progressive pathogenesis, inflammation or markers (ie, signs) may be present without the athlete reporting a given problem or injury (ie, symptoms) and thus continues to be actively engaged in running practice.<sup>20</sup> As a corollary, the moment at which the injury is reported could therefore be described as “sudden onset” in nature. The results from the present study should be interpreted with this point in mind and considered as a precursor for future studies that may confirm or reject our findings. 

A second limitation includes the generalizability since the population in the present study comprised runners using wearable devices. As this article draws on the context of running as the noncontact sport in question, we acknowledge an athlete’s participation in diferent sports and their exposure to practices will increase the probability of sustaining an overuse or overload injury. In contact sports, for example, the overload injury is more likely to occur than noncontact sports, and vice versa. As this is well known, we emphasize that the debate to be had here is not that diferent sports are associated with diferent injury types. Rather, it is within the noncontact sport milieu, such as running. In this context, we may be reframing more injuries through the sudden-onset lens, and fewer through the gradual-onset lens. The important overarching message could be that other noncontact, repetitive action sports could also confirm or refute the hypothesis outlined in this study, leaving implications for coaches of other disciplines. On this basis, more studies in various athletic populations should be conducted to shed light on the “gradual” theory in other contexts than running.

## KEY POINTS

FINDINGS: Overload injury based on an acute mechanism with a sudden onset accounted for 467 of 1666 injuries (28%). The remaining 1199 were classified as overuse injuries (72%). Of these, 1065 of the runners did not report any problem in the same anatomical location as the injury occurred in 28 days prior to injury occurrence. This suggests nearly 90% of overuse injuries to be classified as being based on a repetitive mechanism with a sudden onset, whereas 134 overuse injuries (11.2%) to be based on a repetitive mechanism with a gradual onset. 

IMPLICATIONS: Clinicians, coaches, and runners should consider that most overuse injuries seem to occur more suddenly than the “gradual” approach that has been described in textbooks. Researchers should consider how to calculate progression in training load prior to examining if sudden spikes in load leads to overuse injury. CAUTION: Since the approach used to define a gradual onset of overuse-related injury based on pre-existing problems prior to injury occurrence remains to be validated, the results from the present study should be interpreted with caution.
