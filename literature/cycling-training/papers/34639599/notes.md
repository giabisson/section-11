## Why it belongs

Provides broader context for the small cycling-specific HRV experiment and exposes methodological choices an application would need to preserve.

## Findings — full paper

Eight studies included 199 participants (120 male, 79 female), ranging from untrained to trained, with two cycling studies. Interventions lasted 2–8 weeks. HRV-guided training improved some cardiac-vagal indices, but performance superiority was not established. Endurance performance: SMD 0.20, 95% CI −0.09 to 0.48, p = .18. Maximal oxygen uptake (SMD 0.13) and maximal aerobic power/speed (SMD 0.20) are distinct endpoints and must not be conflated.

## Interpretation and limits

Studies varied in recording posture and duration, baseline, HRV index, averaging, reference-range updates, and thresholds. Selection and detection bias were concerns, and the small evidence base limits conclusions about an optimal method. Favorable physiological HRV changes are not proof of better competition performance. The paper also discusses non-simple relationships between HRV, resting heart rate, and adaptation.

**Reasonable coaching judgment:** make HRV optional contextual evidence. Preserve device/method, date, individual baseline, sufficient observations, and the exact index. A rolling trend may be useful, but no single baseline or cutoff here is validated for every athlete.

**Not justified:** a scientifically validated composite readiness index, universal low-HRV cancellation rules, or using normal/high HRV to dismiss symptoms.
