# Coach takeaways: Llanos-Lagos et al. (2026 issue; online 2025): heavy strength training

**Paper:** Heavy strength training effects on physiological determinants of endurance cyclist performance: a systematic review with meta-analysis.

[Study / DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-025-05883-2) · [Detailed assessment](notes.md) · [All paper takeaways](../../TAKEAWAYS.md)

**Evidence basis:** Full paper assessed; selected original-PDF figures, tables or equations checked. See the detailed assessment for scope and unresolved issues. Prepared 22 October 2026.

## Main coaching lesson

**Consider strength training as part of the athlete's total training budget.**

## What the paper adds

Seventeen studies included 262 cyclists, 60 female, over 5–25 weeks. Heavy strength training was associated with benefits for efficiency, anaerobic power and some cycling-performance outcomes, without clear improvement in maximal oxygen uptake or maximal metabolic steady state. The authors rated certainty as low across outcomes. Time-trial and time-to-exhaustion tests were among the pooled performance measures.

## Coaching application

These are practical coaching interpretations, not a directly tested decision algorithm.

- Discuss strength training in relation to goals, prior lifting experience, technique, equipment, available time and preferences.
- Introduce and progress work according to the athlete's preparation, while monitoring soreness, recovery and the quality of cycling sessions.
- Account for strength work when planning demanding cycling sessions and reviewing the total load.
- Assess whether the combination remains sustainable and useful; decide explicitly whether strength work adds to or replaces other training.

## Limits on the takeaway

The evidence supports a possible useful addition, not a guaranteed improvement or one optimal routine. Study doses varied, and additional training is not the same comparison as replacing cycling within fixed hours. The paper's practical routine suggestions are author interpretation. A standardized effect size is not a percentage performance gain, and improvement need not appear as a higher oxygen-uptake value.

## Implication for a coaching application

Track strength sessions alongside cycling and include them in availability and recovery decisions. Keep the expected benefit and adjustment criteria clear instead of scheduling strength work as a cost-free extra. These are proposed requirements, not claims about implemented repository behavior.
