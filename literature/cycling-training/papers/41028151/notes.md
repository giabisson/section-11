## Why it belongs — additional supporting paper

Directly relevant to adaptive cycling coaching: a recent experiment comparing HRV-only guidance with guidance that also considers well-being and resting heart rate. It complements the existing HRV literature but **does not retrieve or reproduce the unavailable 2019 Javaloyes study**. It is retained as a second user-supplied supporting record alongside the original 15-paper selection.

## Findings — full paper, user-supplied Markdown and original PDF

Participants were randomly allocated among HRV-only, HRV + well-being, and HRV + well-being + resting-heart-rate groups. The program comprised nine baseline days and 31 intervention days. The 28 male completers were experienced cyclists, generally training about 9–11 hours/week. All groups received guided training; there was **no conventional fixed-plan or unguided control**.

The reported between-group comparisons favored HRV + well-being over HRV-only for one-minute power (Bonferroni p = .025), and the three-marker group over HRV-only for five-minute power (p = .012). These were selective outcomes, not across-the-board superiority. The reported omnibus comparison for FTP percentage change was not significant (p = .099), and Figure 2 shows no significant between-group contrasts for 20-minute power, FTP or FTP/kg. Better within-group results in the three-marker group do not establish the incremental benefit of resting heart rate over HRV + well-being.

## Confidence and limitations

- **Very high attrition:** 116 were eligible/randomized and only 28 completed, leaving 88 noncompleters, approximately 76%. This is a calculation from the paper's totals. Random allocation does not remove selection bias in such a small completer sample. The stated dropout reasons (23 illness/injury, 66 voluntary discontinuation, 2 timing exclusions) sum to 91 rather than 88; overlap is not clarified.
- Devices and recording conditions varied: overnight Oura/Whoop measures versus morning seated app/chest-band recordings. Training recommendations were broad High/Low/Rest categories with autonomous execution, rather than identical prescribed workouts. Tests were self-administered indoors or outdoors. This is useful field context but limits control and replication.
- Twenty-minute power, derived FTP and FTP/kg are related measures, not independent confirmations of benefit. The experiment does not validate FTP as a measured physiological threshold, demonstrate an optimal multi-month plan, or test prevention of overtraining syndrome.
- The main text reports unusually large Cohen's d values (9.72 and 6.47). They are present in the original PDF, but were **not independently reproduced** from participant data; do not promote them as reliable benefit magnitudes without re-analysis.
- The custom well-being formula is sleep quality minus fatigue, soreness and stress, each rated 1–7. That formula implies a range of −20 to +4; the original paper states −20 to +10. This is an arithmetic inconsistency, not silently corrected here. It does not validate an arbitrary application's composite readiness score.

## Practical implication

**Evidence-supported, cautiously:** selected short-power outcomes in this small experiment favored combining observations over HRV alone. This adds a direct test to the broader rationale for collecting subjective feedback.

**Reasonable coaching judgment:** consider HRV, resting heart rate, symptoms and well-being together, using consistent individual measurements and explicit uncertainty. A normal wearable result should not override illness or pain.

**Not established:** general superiority for endurance/FTP outcomes, a causal extra benefit of resting heart rate over HRV + well-being, validated illness/injury prediction, or a transferable readiness cutoff.

Supplementary Materials A (decision rules) and B (individual changes) and the linked OSF dataset were not supplied or reviewed. Exact algorithm replication, effect-size verification and missing-data re-analysis therefore remain open; no application algorithm was changed.
