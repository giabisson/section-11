## Why it belongs

The most directly relevant synthesis here for distinguishing session programming from a training plan that spans weeks and seasons.

## Findings — full paper, manually supplied

Seven studies included 161 cyclists, only 10 women. Most comparative interventions lasted 8–12 weeks; a single-athlete block-periodization case lasted 58 weeks. Traditional and block approaches, and monitoring-led day-by-day programming, produced improvements without a consistently superior model. Reported volumes were roughly 7.5–10.8 h/week for traditional approaches and 8.8–11.7 h/week for block approaches.

## Interpretation and limits

The authors distinguish short-term programming from genuine seasonal periodization. An elite single-athlete case does not resolve comparative seasonal effectiveness. The review labels studies low risk using a modified PEDro approach, but small samples, nonrandomized evidence, and heterogeneous volume and intensity still limit confidence. The paper's own practical 7–12 h/week suggestion is author judgment for trained nonelite cyclists, not a tested universal minimum.

Some review descriptions of the HRV studies overstate the inference from within-group improvements. The directly available 2020 study reports no clear between-group performance difference; see the [separate note](../31490431/notes.md). The reported average intensity percentages on printed p. 117 do not sum to 100% when combined as listed; they should not become a normalized plan recipe. The flow diagram also has inconsistent screening labels/counts, so its numbers should not be used as a clean screening audit.

**Reasonable coaching judgment:** maintain block objectives, event demands, feasible progression and recovery, while allowing day-to-day adjustment. Autumn alone does not dictate the same phase for everyone.

**Not justified:** one optimal annual model, a universal 3:1 loading cycle, or fixed load ramps.
