# Cycling coaching: literature foundation

Prepared 22 October 2026. This is a curated reading set for a **general cycling coach**, covering workout design, planning, adaptation, and recovery. It is not a prescribed training plan or a systematic review. The initial example of three indoor sessions and 3–4 hours per week is an application context, not an inclusion restriction. Implementation status is recorded in the [repository coaching update plan](../../COACHING_UPDATE_PLAN.md).

**Access:** the original 15-paper selection plus two separately supplied supporting papers (17 records total). Sixteen have available full text and 14 have saved original PDFs. The original 2019 Javaloyes paper remains abstract-only: the supplied HRV file is a different 2020 study. Five previously missing core papers now have full text and original PDFs. See [manual download list](UNAVAILABLE.md), [access and verification manifest](access-status.json), [search log](search-log.json), and [BibTeX](bibliography.bib). Per-paper notes distinguish direct findings, limitations, and coaching interpretation. These notes are the synthesis; the saved abstracts are source material. The five newly available core-paper assessments supersede their earlier abstract-only versions.

## Additional user-supplied supporting papers

[Javaloyes et al. (2020), HRV versus block periodization](papers/31490431/notes.md), DOI `10.1519/JSC.0000000000003337`, is now stored separately. It differs from the still-missing 2019 paper in the table. It reports 15 completers from 20 recruited and no clear between-group fitness/performance advantage. It is already represented in the HRV review evidence base, so it is not counted as an independent new replication. [Import provenance](manual-import.json) records all seven renamed files.

[Alfonso et al. (2025), HRV, resting heart rate and well-being](papers/41028151/notes.md), DOI `10.1038/s41598-025-13540-z`, is a relevant recent experiment. Selected one- and five-minute power comparisons favored combined guidance over HRV alone. Very high attrition (28 of 116 eligible participants completed), no fixed-plan control, inconsistent score reporting and unavailable supplementary rules limit transfer to an application. It does not establish the added benefit of resting heart rate over HRV plus well-being, general FTP superiority or a validated readiness index. It supports further evaluation of combined monitoring, not automatic adoption of its algorithm.

## Implications for a generic coach

### Evidence-supported practice, with limits

- **Organize training beyond individual sessions, without claiming one periodization model is best.** The cycling review found only seven eligible studies and no favored model over 8–12 weeks; seasonal comparisons were scarce. This supports humility about a fixed annual template, not abandoning planning. [Galán-Rioja](papers/36640771/notes.md), full paper; comparative interventions were mostly short, with a separate 58-week single-athlete case.
- **Allow more than one sensible intensity distribution.** The participant-data meta-analysis found no clear overall advantage for polarized over pyramidal training. Training level may modify responses, but neither competitive nor recreational subgroup alone established a significant winner. Session classification and actual time in physiological zones are different quantities. [Rosenblat](papers/39888556/notes.md), full paper, Figures 3–4.
- **Use intervals as one useful stimulus.** Several cycling studies show improvements with interval training, but dose, training status, comparator, and study duration matter. An acute physiological response or a two-week molecular adaptation does not establish long-term superiority or equivalence to long-ride preparation. [Seiler](papers/21812820/notes.md) and [Rønnestad](papers/24382021/notes.md), full papers with protocol and reporting caveats; [Little](papers/20100740/notes.md), full text without PDF.
- **Observe both training and the athlete's response.** Validated subjective well-being measures can be responsive to training stress. HRV can provide additional information when measurement and baseline methods are consistent; clear performance superiority of HRV-driven prescription is not established. [Saw](papers/26423706/notes.md), full text; [Manresa-Rocamora](papers/34639599/notes.md), full paper. The newer [Alfonso trial](papers/41028151/notes.md) reports selective short-power benefits of combined monitoring over HRV alone, with major limitations and no fixed-plan comparator.
- **Manage recovery and investigate persistent problems.** The ECSS/ACSM consensus emphasizes individualized load, recovery, non-training stress, nutrition, illness, and performance. No single marker diagnoses overtraining syndrome. Its guidance is expert consensus informed by research, not validation of an automated diagnostic score. [Meeusen](papers/23247672/notes.md), full paper, pp. 188–201.
- **Match evaluation to the target performance.** FTP, maximal oxygen uptake, fresh short efforts, and performance after prolonged work describe different aspects of capacity. Do not interpret them as interchangeable physiological thresholds or complete event-readiness measures. [Wong](papers/36803419/notes.md), full paper with a specific FTP protocol and a 40-minute test cap; [Rosenblat](papers/39888556/notes.md) and [Sánchez-Jiménez](papers/40613880/notes.md), full papers.
- **Offer strength work as an option with a time and recovery cost.** A recent cycling meta-analysis found benefits for some performance outcomes, with low certainty of evidence. It does not establish one optimal strength routine for every cyclist. [Llanos-Lagos](papers/40632222/notes.md), full paper.
- **Avoid making carbohydrate restriction a default performance strategy.** A nine-study meta-analysis did not establish a performance advantage; this does not prove it can never help anyone. It also does not supply a complete fueling prescription. [Gejl & Nybo](papers/34001184/notes.md), full paper, Figure 2.
- **Consider an individualized taper for an important event.** The taper review supports performance improvements, but several commonly quoted “optimal” settings come from small subgroups without significant differences between settings. Event tapering is not evidence for a mandatory recovery week every fourth week. [Wang](papers/37163550/notes.md), full paper, Figures 3–4 and Table 2.

### Reasonable coaching judgment to evaluate in the application

These are design interpretations of the evidence, not directly tested algorithms:

1. Maintain an athlete profile with event demands, experience, recent completed training, current fitness/test provenance, availability, preferences, and constraints. Plan toward a target or general fitness goal; autumn alone should not force one phase for every athlete.
2. Give each training block an objective, a feasible weekly structure, a progression hypothesis, and a review date. Progress one or a few variables when completion and response support it; do not require an automatic weekly percentage increase.
3. Close the loop: compare prescribed versus completed sessions, ask why sessions changed, consider effort and recovery, and revise future training. A missed session is not automatically a fitness loss or a debt to be repaid.
4. Keep easy, moderate, and hard work explicit, with the zone model named. Choose the amount and placement of demanding work around total load, goals, training history, and recovery. Do not scale an elite rider's hours or intensity ratios mechanically to another athlete.
5. Use missing or contradictory observations to lower confidence and ask focused questions. Illness, injury, persistent fatigue, or falling performance should interrupt routine progression and may warrant clinical assessment; they should not be averaged away by a favorable readiness score.

### Claims this set does not justify

- A universally optimal 80/20 split, 4×8 session, short-interval format, fixed three-week build/one-week recovery pattern, or fixed weekly load ramp.
- Treating FTP as identical to lactate threshold, critical power, or maximal metabolic steady state; converting a studied percentage of ramp peak power directly into the same percentage of FTP.
- A composite readiness index with validated cutoffs merely because it includes HRV, resting heart rate, sleep, or wellness.
- Predicting illness/injury from a single load number, or diagnosing overtraining syndrome from a wearable.
- Claiming that HIT replaces all event-specific endurance work, that fasted training is necessary, or that a plan is scientifically proven because it contains studied sessions.

## Scope, access, and reuse

The selection deliberately mixes synthesis papers, informative cycling experiments, and one authoritative consensus. Reviews and included primary experiments overlap; they are not independent replications to be added together. Most underlying samples are small and disproportionately male. Female athletes, masters, novices, long-term adherence, indoor heat/cooling, pain/injury management, current illness return-to-sport guidance, and detailed fueling doses need further targeted evidence before corresponding algorithms are designed. This collection is not a complete clinical or nutrition guideline.

The PubMed searches and metadata were retrieved on 22 October 2026. Broad queries sometimes produced irrelevant records or warnings that phrase clauses were dropped; the search log retains those results. Titles, abstracts, and relevance were manually checked for the final set. There was no exhaustive screening, preregistration, or independent second reviewer. Absence from this collection means “not assessed,” not “no evidence exists.” Issue dates and online dates differ for several papers and are preserved in the metadata.

Each `papers/<PMID>/` contains metadata, the retrieved abstract, and original review notes. `access-status.json` records retrieval outcomes, source URLs, complete ETH pagination, PDF hashes, and visual checks. [PDF verification notes](PDF-VERIFICATION.md) record discrepancies and equations that could otherwise be misread. [References](REFERENCES.md) provide formatted citations.

`sources/<PMID>/` contains local research copies: available PDFs, extracted full text, the seven renamed MinerU Markdown files, raw retrieval responses, and selected page renders. MinerU image links still point to its remote service; the matching PDFs are saved locally so those links are not required for original-figure access. It is intentionally Git-ignored: publisher material is not covered by this project's license and is not being redistributed with the repository. The tracked notes and manifest remain usable without those copies; a clone will need to retrieve them again. An ETH network connection was confirmed, but that alone does not establish journal entitlement. Blocked responses were reported, with no access controls bypassed.
